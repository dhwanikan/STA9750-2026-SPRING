Introduction

Nobody knew exactly what would happen. That was the honest answer in June 2023, when the Supreme Court struck down race-conscious admissions in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. Advocates on both sides made confident predictions — catastrophic drops in Black and Hispanic enrollment on one hand, barely a ripple on the other. The truth, as usual, was going to require actual data.

That data is now here. Fall 2024 marks the first full admissions cycle conducted entirely under post-SFFA rules, and IPEDS — the federal clearinghouse for college enrollment statistics — has released the numbers.

This project works through those numbers systematically. Using 15 years of enrollment records (2010–2024), I examine how racial composition has shifted at institutions across the country, paying close attention to CUNY schools and California’s public universities. The California schools are a natural control group: Proposition 209 banned affirmative action there in 1996, so their demographic trends tell us what campuses look like when admissions have been race-blind for decades. Comparing other institutions against that benchmark is the cleanest way to separate genuine SFFA effects from background noise.

Data Acquisition and Preparation

Task 1: Acquire IPEDS Data

The following function downloads fall enrollment (EF) and institution description (HD) files from IPEDS for 2010–2024. Files are cached locally in data/mp01/ to avoid repeated downloads.

Code 
library(tidyverse)
library(glue)

acquire_ipeds_data <- function(start_year=2010, end_year=2024){
    data_dir <- file.path("data", "mp01")
    if(!dir.exists(data_dir)){
        dir.create(data_dir, showWarnings=FALSE, recursive=TRUE)
    }
    YEARS <- seq(start_year, end_year)

    EFA_ALL <- map(YEARS, function(yy){
        if(yy <= 2022){
            ef_url <- glue("https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/datacenter/data/EF{yy}A.zip")
        } else {
            ef_url <- glue("https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/data-generator?year={yy}&tableName=EF{yy}A&HasRV=0&type=csv")
        }
        ef_file <- file.path(data_dir, glue("ef{yy}a.csv.zip"))
        if(!file.exists(ef_file)){
            message(glue("Downloading Enrollment Data for {yy}"))
            download.file(ef_url, destfile=ef_file, quiet=TRUE, mode="wb")
        }
        read_csv(ef_file, show_col_types=FALSE) |>
            mutate(year = yy,
                   enrollment_m_aian = EFAIANM, enrollment_f_aian = EFAIANW,
                   enrollment_m_asia = EFASIAM, enrollment_f_asia = EFASIAW,
                   enrollment_m_bkaa = EFBKAAM, enrollment_f_bkaa = EFBKAAW,
                   enrollment_m_hisp = EFHISPM, enrollment_f_hisp = EFHISPW,
                   enrollment_m_nhpi = EFNHPIM, enrollment_f_nhpi = EFNHPIW,
                   enrollment_m_whit = EFWHITM, enrollment_f_whit = EFWHITW,
                   enrollment_m_2mor = EF2MORM, enrollment_f_2mor = EF2MORW,
                   enrollment_m_unkn = EFUNKNM, enrollment_f_unkn = EFUNKNW,
                   enrollment_m_nral = EFNRALM, enrollment_f_nral = EFNRALW) |>
            filter((EFALEVEL %in% c(2, 12)) | (LINE %in% c(1, 15))) |>
            mutate(level = case_when(
                EFALEVEL == 2  ~ "all undergrad",
                EFALEVEL == 12 ~ "all graduate",
                LINE %in% c(1, 15) ~ "first year undergrad"
            )) |>
            select(institution_id = UNITID, year, level,
                   starts_with("enrollment_")) |>
            group_by(institution_id, year, level) |>
            summarize(across(starts_with("enrollment_"), sum), .groups="drop")
    }) |> bind_rows()

    DESC_ALL <- map(YEARS, function(yy){
        if(yy <= 2022){
            hd_url <- glue("https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/datacenter/data/HD{yy}.zip")
        } else {
            hd_url <- glue("https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/data-generator?year={yy}&tableName=HD{yy}&HasRV=0&type=csv")
        }
        hd_file <- file.path(data_dir, glue("hd{yy}.csv.zip"))
        if(!file.exists(hd_file)){
            message(glue("Downloading Institution Descriptions for {yy}"))
            download.file(hd_url, destfile=hd_file, quiet=TRUE, mode="wb")
        }
        suppressWarnings(
            read_csv(hd_file, show_col_types=FALSE,
                     locale=locale(encoding=if_else(yy==2024,"utf-8","windows-1252"))) |>
            mutate(year=yy) |>
            select(institution_id=UNITID, institution_name=INSTNM,
                   state=STABBR, year)
        )
    }) |> bind_rows()

    inner_join(EFA_ALL, DESC_ALL, join_by(institution_id, year))
}

IPEDS <- acquire_ipeds_data()

One important methodological note before proceeding: before attributing any 2024 demographic shift to SFFA, we should account for the COVID-19 disruption. Baruch’s first-year diversity rose by 3.0% between 2019 and 2020 as the pandemic reshaped who applied and enrolled. By 2022, diversity had recovered and exceeded pre-pandemic levels at 39.2%. This COVID baseline effect means that simple 2023-to-2024 comparisons may conflate pandemic recovery with genuine SFFA effects — a subtlety the California comparison helps control for, since California schools experienced the same COVID disruption without any change in admissions policy.

        total    = sum(enrollment_total),
        .groups  = "drop"

    filter(total >= 1000) |>
    mutate(
        q_aian     = aian     / total,
        q_asia     = asia     / total,
        q_bkaa     = bkaa     / total,

             P[["asia"]]     * log(P[["asia"]]     / (q_asia     + 1e-10)) +

             P[["whit"]]     * log(P[["whit"]]     / (q_whit     + 1e-10)) +

# Extra Credit: Shannon entropy diversity metric
# H = -sum(p * log(p)) across all racial groups
# Higher entropy = more evenly distributed = more diverse
# Nugget term 1e-10 handles p=0 without producing NA

compute_entropy <- function(enrollment_row) {
    groups <- c(
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_aian"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_aian"]],
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_asia"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_asia"]],
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_bkaa"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_bkaa"]],
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_hisp"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_hisp"]],
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_nhpi"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_nhpi"]],
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_whit"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_whit"]],
        enrollment_row[["enrollment_m_2mor"]] + enrollment_row[["enrollment_f_2mor"]]
    )
    total <- sum(groups)
    if (total == 0) return(NA_real_)
    props <- groups / total
    -sum(props * log(props + 1e-10))
}

The following Op-Ed is written from the perspective of the President of Baruch College for publication in the campus newspaper.

One Year After SFFA: What Our Numbers Actually Show
By the President of Baruch College

Last June marked one year since the Supreme Court’s ruling in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard reshaped college admissions across the country. Commentators predicted sweeping demographic shifts. Now that the data is in, it’s time to share what we’re actually seeing at Baruch — and what it means.

For this analysis, I measure diversity using Shannon entropy — a statistical measure borrowed from information theory that captures how evenly enrollment is distributed across all racial groups simultaneously, rather than collapsing everything into a single “percent minority” figure. A higher entropy means a more genuinely balanced student body; a lower entropy means enrollment is concentrated in fewer groups. By this measure, Baruch’s entering class had an entropy of 1.35 in Fall 2024, compared to 1.343 the year before and 1.345 in 2022. The year-over-year shift was 0.007 upward. Using the simpler “percent of color” metric, our incoming class of 2,532 first-year students was 40.9% students of color in Fall 2024, compared to 40.9% the year before and 39.2% in 2022.

Looking beyond the entering class to Baruch’s entire undergraduate student body tells a similar story. Across all enrolled undergraduates, 40.3% identified as students of color in Fall 2024, compared to 39.6% the prior year — a shift of 0.8% upward. Because the full student body reflects multiple entering cohorts, it changes more slowly than the entering class alone — making even small year-over-year shifts meaningful signals.

To put this in context, consider California’s public universities — institutions that have operated without race-conscious admissions since Proposition 209 passed in 1996, nearly three decades before SFFA. They represent our best available baseline for what “normal” year-to-year fluctuation looks like in the complete absence of race-conscious policy.

At UC Berkeley, first-year class diversity changed by 0.6% upward between 2023 and 2024. At Cal State LA — a large urban commuter school whose mission more closely resembles Baruch’s than Berkeley’s does — the shift was 1.3% upward. Baruch’s change of 0.0% sits within that California range — suggesting our shift is consistent with the normal variation seen even at schools entirely unaffected by the ruling.

Zooming out further: since 2010, Baruch’s first-year diversity has shifted by 23.3% upward in total — a long-term trend that dwarfs any single-year post-SFFA movement and reflects the changing demographics of New York City itself rather than any admissions policy.

These numbers tell only part of the story. Baruch has always derived its strength from serving New York City — one of the most diverse cities on Earth. Our students come from over 150 countries, speak dozens of languages, and represent every borough. That diversity is written into our mission and has not changed because of a Supreme Court ruling.

We will continue to pursue diversity through every permissible avenue: outreach to underserved communities, partnerships with NYC high schools, robust financial aid, and a campus culture that actively welcomes first-generation college students. The Court told us how we can pursue diversity — not whether we should.

The data will continue to evolve as more post-SFFA admissions cycles complete. We are committed to transparency, and will share our numbers every year. Baruch’s doors remain open — and our commitment to reflecting the full breadth of New York City remains unchanged.
